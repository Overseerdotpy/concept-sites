The brief

Every GPU cloud looks like the same GPU cloud.

Obsidra is a fictional company I invented to practice end-to-end brand and product design. Its pitch is simple: rent high-end GPUs with no waitlist, no sales call and no minimum term, and pay only for the seconds you use.

Looking at the space, almost every competitor shares one visual language: a near-black page, an electric cyan or blue accent, a geometric sans and a "Contact sales" button. That sameness was the real problem to solve. If Obsidra looked like the category, its genuinely different pricing would get lost in it.

The category default

  • Cyan or electric blue on black
  • Geometric sans everywhere
  • Pricing hidden behind a form
  • Claims about speed, never proof

What Obsidra needed to say

  • Premium and precise, not generic
  • Prices on the page
  • Per-second billing you can see
  • Built for engineers, not procurement

The name

Named after the sharpest edge on Earth.

Obsidian is volcanic glass. Fractured, it forms an edge finer than surgical steel, and when light hits it you see a hidden spectral sheen. Both ideas became the brand's foundation: precision in the type, and a dark surface that catches color in the palette.

Color

Three directions, one deliberate choice.

I explored three palettes on the same mini hero. Volcanic glass told the obsidian story well, but its molten orange would have clashed with Amberline, the next brand in this series. Cold silicon was well executed and completely expected.

Spectral sheen won because it is still literal to obsidian, it stands apart from the cyan everyone else uses, and its mint secondary has a real job in the product: it means healthy.

Typography

A serif on an AI company, on purpose.

My first direction used Archivo at 125% width: bold, engineered, safe. The second, which I called Scalpel, used Bodoni Moda, a high-contrast Didone whose hairline strokes look literally sharp.

I chose Scalpel. No GPU cloud uses a serif, so it is instantly distinctive, and it repositions the brand closer to a precision instrument than a developer tool. The risk is legibility, so I wrote rules to contain it.

Direction B · Scalpel Chosen Rent H100s by the second. Precise, premium, unlike anything nearby.

Serif stops at 27px

Hairlines break up below that on dark screens, so h3 and smaller switch to Hanken Grotesk.

Numbers get a mono

Prices, latency and counts use Spline Sans Mono with tabular figures, so live values never jitter.

No serif in the product

Marketing speaks in the brand's voice. The dashboard is for scanning, so it stays sans and mono.

Wordmark

Capitals, used exactly once.

The wordmark is set in tracked capitals, the classic language of fine instruments and fashion houses. Headlines stay in sentence case, because all-caps text reads slower and would make the logo stop feeling special.

Like real luxury marks, it has two cuts: a lighter one for large sizes and a heavier, wider one for the nav, where thin hairlines would otherwise disappear.

Landing page

Don't describe per-second billing. Run it.

Every competitor says "pay only for what you use." I wanted visitors to watch it happen. The hero's terminal types a real deploy command, brings eight GPUs online one at a time, and then starts a live meter: the clock and the cost tick up together, every second.

It's the only motion on the page, so it gets all the attention. Visitors who prefer reduced motion see a finished snapshot instead, and screen readers get one plain sentence rather than a number changing every second.

Product

The dashboard keeps the landing page's promise.

The cluster screen picks up the same run the hero started. The goal was to make the moment the meter stops feel as trustworthy as the moment it starts.

In the dashboard, color is never decoration. Each hue has one meaning, so a glance at the GPU grid tells you whether anything needs attention.

Craft

The details nobody notices until they're missing.

AA contrast throughout. Every text token checked against every surface it sits on, in both themes.

Light and dark themes. Pages follow the visitor's system setting. The terminal stays dark in both, because terminals are.

Reduced motion respected. Animations become finished snapshots for people who ask their OS for less movement.

No layout shift. Animated areas reserve their final height, so nothing on the page jumps.

Real controls. Native buttons and dialogs, so keyboard and screen reader users get the same flows.

Production tokens. The whole system ships as Tailwind v4 CSS variables, ready to drop into a Next.js app.

Reflection

What I'd do next.

This is a concept project, so nothing here has met real users yet. My next step would be testing the stop and scale flows with a few engineers who rent GPUs today, especially whether showing the final cost before confirming builds trust or causes hesitation.

I'd also rebuild the hero visual with a WebGL glass effect, design the docs and usage-and-billing pages, and port everything into Next.js components on the existing token system.
