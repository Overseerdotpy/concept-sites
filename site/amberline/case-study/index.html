The brief

Freelance income is lumpy. The worry comes from not knowing.

Amberline is a fictional company I invented to practice end-to-end product design. Its users are designers, writers, developers and photographers whose income arrives one invoice at a time, often late, never on a schedule.

Most finance tools answer that with a bank's visual language: navy, charts, alerts. But the anxiety isn't about the balance. It's about the money that's somewhere in between: sent, opened, late, maybe coming. So the whole brand is built around one idea: money on its way to you.

Positioning in the series

Designed as Obsidra's opposite.

Amberline is the second brand in a portfolio series. The first, Obsidra, is a dark, cool, serif-led GPU cloud. If Amberline shared any of that, the portfolio would read as one style used twice, so every foundational choice deliberately goes the other way.

Color

The problem with amber: it usually means warning.

An amber brand color risks every button reading as caution, which is a bad feeling next to someone's income. I explored three ways to handle that.

Fossil won: forest green, the color of banknotes and ledgers, does the heavy lifting for actions and paid money. That frees amber for one specific, positive job.

Typography

Chosen for numbers first.

In a money app, amounts in a column have to line up. Before shortlisting fonts, I opened each candidate's font file and checked for tabular figures, digits that are all the same width. Several popular choices failed.

Familjen Grotesk passed with a bonus: its digits are tabular by default, so alignment can't be forgotten. Obsidra chose type for character; Amberline chose it for numbers.

Logo

The l is the line.

The "l" in "line" is set in solid amber: the line money travels along to reach you. It took four rounds to get right, and two of them were mistakes worth keeping.

01 · First build

Too heavy, wrong "a"

I sized the amber bar from the l's outer bounds, which include a small foot. It came out 40% thicker than every other stroke.

02 · Concept in Leonardo

It reads as a "d"

Fusing a tall stem onto an "a" makes the exact shape of a "d". The fix was keeping the stem at the a's height.

03 · Revised concept

The monogram

A round single-story "a" with an amber stem. Right idea, but AI images blur when scaled and can't be edited.

04 · Vector rebuild

Cut from the real letters

Familjen has no single-story "a", so I cut one from its "d". Its stem matches the l's exactly, which made the animation possible.

Final wordmark: every stroke the same weight, the "a" matching the monogram.

First version, for comparison: the amber l is visibly heavier.

Motion

The stem leaves the "a" and becomes the "l".

Because the a's stem and the l are the same width, the logo can animate its own idea: the amber stem flows along the word and lands as the "l". I built three versions in code from the outlined letters.

Rainbow version, running live

Rainbow

Landing page

The bounce means money arrived.

The l's bounce became the brand's one playful gesture, and it only ever happens when money lands. By the end of the page, visitors have learned what it means without being told.

The rest of the page follows the same rule: every animation shows the product doing its job. A live card sends, reminds and gets paid; a horizontal scroll carries one invoice from draft to paid; a calculator splits any amount into taxes and take-home.

Product

The dashboard answers one question: where's my money?

The home screen leads with what's coming, what's paid, what's in the tax pot and what's overdue, then shows the month as one bar split by the four money states, the same colors as everywhere else.

Every action works: send a draft, remind a late client, mark an invoice paid, or create a new one. Each change updates every total, moves 25% into the tax pot, logs the activity, and bounces the logo in the sidebar.

Craft

The details behind it.

AA contrast throughout. Bright amber is never used for text; a deep amber handles amber-colored words.

Real logo files. Outlined from the font with its kerning, in seven versions with usage rules.

Reduced motion respected. Every animation has a finished, still state.

Quiet for screen readers. Live demos announce one plain sentence, not every changing number.

Light and dark themes. Both follow the visitor's system setting.

Honest fine print. The tax calculator says it's an estimate, not advice.

Reflection

What I'd do next.

This is a concept, so nothing has met real users yet. I'd test the reminder tones with freelancers who chase payments today, and whether "On its way" actually lowers anxiety compared with "Pending".

The biggest lesson was the logo: AI tools were great for finding the idea fast, but the real work was rebuilding it precisely from the letterforms, which is what made the motion possible.
