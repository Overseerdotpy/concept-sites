The brief

The fear isn't fitness. It's "am I ready?"

Northvale is a fictional guiding company I invented: hut-to-hut treks, first glacier crossings and beginner summits in the Alps, in groups of six. Its customers have hiked plenty but never gone above the tree line with ropes and crampons.

They don't doubt their legs. They doubt whether they belong up there. So every page has one job: make a scary-sounding trip knowable, day by day, step by step, until booking feels like the obvious next move.

Photography

One shoot, generated.

The brand lives on mountain photography, so I art-directed a twelve-shot set in Leonardo. Every prompt ended with the same style line, so the light, color, and grain match like one photographer's work.

Two tricks kept it believable: people appear small or from behind, since AI can't keep a face consistent, and guides always wear a rust-red jacket. That thread later became the brand color.

Retouching

Light comes from the left.

The hero photo had a flaw that's easy to miss: long shadows floating down the left-hand slope, attached to no one, on the side of the ridge that's in full sun.

Measuring the snow settled it. The left slope is sunlit, the right is in shade, so the climbers' shadows must fall right, onto snow that's already shaded, where they all but disappear. I removed the false shadows, restored the film grain, and cast each climber's silhouette away from the sun. Drag to compare.

Color

Rust is the guide's color.

The palette was pulled straight from the photos: snow, granite, glacier blue, and the guides' rust. The page stays nearly neutral so the photography leads.

One rule holds it together: rust only ever means "this way", the next step, the route, the button. Scarcity is ochre, calm rather than alarming, so "2 seats left" informs without pressuring nervous beginners.

Typography

Signs, not sentences.

Barlow Condensed comes from road and trail signage. Set in capitals it reads like the waymarkers hikers already trust, and its narrow figures pack trip data into tight spaces. Regular Barlow carries every sentence.

Logo

The vale, then the peak.

In the wordmark, the A is a mountain set right after the V, so the name dips into the valley and climbs to the summit. The standalone mark mirrors that peak in a still lake, and the two halves form a compass needle whose red end points north.

Motion

The needle finds north.

On a first visit, the mark turns solid, spins in 3D like a compass needle, settles facing you, and becomes the wordmark: the reflection fades and the peak narrows into the A as the letters rise. The site's footer ends on the same needle settling.

Landing page

Make the trip knowable.

The page answers the fear in order. A draggable elevation profile walks the trip day by day: how far, how high, where you sleep, what you'll learn. A four-question quiz recommends a starting trip, and if someone isn't ready, it gives them an eight-week plan instead of a no.

Then the guides: their photo drains to grey except the rust jacket. Follow the red jacket is both a brand line and how you'd actually find your guide on a busy glacier.

The four-day glacier crossing as one line: 38.4 km, +2,140 m, 3,312 m high point, with every number matching the trip card.

Product

Booking with no dead ends.

Five steps, a route line for progress, and a live summary that prices every choice. Nothing turns a person away: a full date becomes a waitlist, and "not yet" on the fitness check leads to a training plan, not a refusal.

After paying, the trip dashboard takes over: a countdown, a "before you go" checklist, a hello from your guide, the itinerary, the training plan, and a packing list that remembers what you've packed.

Craft

The details behind it.

Numbers that agree. Trip cards, the route, the hero, and the dashboard share one set of distances, climbs and dates.

Keyboard throughout. The route profile is a real slider: arrows walk it, Page Up jumps a day.

Plain-language errors. "Card numbers here are 16 digits. You've entered 15."

Reduced motion respected. The intro, mist, spin, and color drain all have still versions.

Reflection

What I learned.

AI photography is fast, but it needs an art director's eye. The shadow error looked right at a glance; it took checking where the light came from to see it was wrong. I now check every generated image for shadows, logos and complete files before it goes near a layout.

Next, I'd test the "Am I ready?" quiz with real first-time alpinists, and whether showing seats left as calm ochre still drives bookings.
